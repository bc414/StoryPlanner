The Root Pathology: Ontological Promiscuity The underlying problem is not a filing problem. It is an *ontological collision* problem: notes about the same /subject/ (Chrysalis, Aquileia) are stored together regardless of what /kind of claim/ each note is making. A psychological observation, a macroeconomic mechanic, a chronological event, and a craft note about when to reveal something to the reader are all fundamentally different kinds of propositions. They require completely different analytical lenses, different editorial questions, and different uses during drafting. When they cohabit the same entity, none of them can be reviewed coherently. This is not unique to this fabula — it is the near-universal failure mode of large-scale worldbuilding projects. The topic becomes the organizational unit instead of the /type of claim being made about the topic/. The symptom: scanning through a character entity like "Queen Chrysalis" forces the author to constantly context-switch between being a psychologist, a historian, an economist, a military analyst, and a narrative strategist within the same scroll. The cognitive overhead is enormous, and errors of omission pile up because a note about her epistemological blind spots will be lost in a sea of timeline entries. ------------------------------------------------------------------------ What Professional Authors Actually Do Professional authors working at this scale — Martin, Tolkien, Sanderson, Le Guin, Hobb — maintain multiple /distinct document types/ that correspond to distinct analytical purposes. They do not separate by topic; they separate by *the nature of the claim being made*. The standard professional toolkit at this scale typically includes: *1. The Character Bible (Psychological Architecture)* Purely about interiority. Core wound, desire, fear, worldview, epistemological limits, arc shape. No events, no world mechanics. The /only/ question it answers is: /Who is this creature, and why?/ This is the document that gets consulted when writing a chapter to ensure every decision the character makes is authentic to their psychological architecture. *2. The World Bible / Lore Documents (Objective Fabula Truth)* The externally verifiable facts of the world: how the economy functions, what the magic physics are, what nations exist and how they interact, what events occurred and when. This is entirely independent of who knows it. The reader and every character may be wrong about it; the document is the canonical ground truth. *3. The Timeline / Chronological Record (Event Sequence)* The fabula in strict temporal order. What happened, when, to whom. This is distinct from the lore because it is not structural — it is sequential. It answers /when/, not /how/. The Great Leap Forward begins in 982; that is a timeline fact. The MEFO bill mechanism is a lore fact. They belong in different documents even though they are related. *4. The Narrative Strategy Document (Syuzhet Architecture)* The most discipline-specific document for fiction: when does the reader know what? What does the POV character observe versus what does the reader infer? Where are the curiosity hooks planted? What is withheld and why? This document is explicitly about the /gap between the fabula and the syuzhet/. It does not appear in Tolkien's appendices or Sanderson's cosmere notes because readers never see it — but it exists in the drafting process. *5. The Thematic Argument Map (Meaning Layer)* What is the story arguing, and how does each structural element serve that argument? This is analytical and directional — it checks that events, characters, and reveals are not just coherent but /purposeful/. The critical professional insight is that these documents are *never collapsed together*, because each one enables a different mode of thinking. You enter the Character Bible to ask "would she do this?" You enter the Lore Document to ask "is this mechanically possible?" Temporal Layer: Fabula Time vs. Syuzhet Time The chronological record of events (Layer 2) is entirely distinct from the order in which those events are presented to the reader. The pre-war history of Chrysalis is fabula time. The way the reader learns fragments of it through Coltbert's paper, Rainbow Dash's infiltration, the drug trade scenes — that is syuzhet time. "Chrysalis personally assumes the Kriemhild form for high-profile humiliation meetings" is a micro-behavioral claim. The macro claim belongs in Nation/System Codex. The micro claim belongs in Character psychology. When they coexist in one note, the reader cannot tell what kind of claim is being made, which makes it hard to stress-test either claim rigorously. 3. The Craft Layer: World Truth vs. Delivery Technique Some notes describe what is true in the world. Other notes describe /how to show that truth effectively/. "Chrysalis is a brilliant orator who inspires changelings" — world truth. "Need to make Chrysalis a good orator (like mustache man) to inspire the changelings" — craft note about technique. These are different. World truths go in Codex/ Character. Craft notes belong in Narrative Strategy / Authorial Directives / Ideas. Mixing them creates a document that is simultaneously a world bible and a writer's notebook, which serves neither function well. 4. The Prequel Calibration Problem (For the Main Story) Because the prequels will eventually dramatize the full backstory, every mention of a prequel character or event in TLTT needs to be calibrated: enough to honor the world's depth and plant curiosity hooks for the prequels, but not enough to spoil them. A separate analytical layer is needed for each prequel-relevant character and event: what is their TLTT surface (the version AJ and Twilight encounter) vs. their full fabula depth. Currently these exist at the same level of resolution in the same notes. 5. MINERVA AT GEOPOLITICAL EPIC SCOPE The Minerva framework is built around five techniques that each solve a single-protagonist, single-conflict problem. Scaling them to a geopolitical epic requires structural extension without discarding the underlying principles. The extensions, matched to each technique: Compelling Conflict → The Nested Goal Hierarchy Minerva's "visible and concrete goal" works for a single protagonist. A geopolitical epic requires a *nested goal hierarchy* where concrete goals at each scale are linked causally, and the failure or success of a small goal visibly changes the difficulty of the goal one level up. The fabula already has this structure: Applejack's battle-level goal (hold the bridge) feeds the operational goal (defend Tall Tale), which feeds the strategic goal (force a stalemate), which feeds the civilizational goal (establish a Republic that makes Equestria permanently defensible). Each level must have its own visible, concrete success condition. The reader must be able to answer "at what point does Applejack need to hold this bridge?" and "what happens to the Republic if she doesn't?" The "character must have no viable alternatives" principle becomes, at the geopolitical scale: *the structural situation must eliminate options at each level simultaneously*. Applejack cannot simply win the battle and leave the civilizational problem unsolved. The design must ensure that the only path to a just and stable peace runs through every level of the hierarchy. This is the structural logic of why the Equestrian Republic cannot simply be declared — it must be earned through a sequence of decisions that close off the alternatives one by one. Progressive Escalation → The Synchronized Ratchet Minerva's escalation principle: each obstacle is measurably harder than the last. In a geopolitical epic with simultaneous tracks, escalation must be *synchronized across all tracks*, meaning no track is allowed to become easier while another escalates. The failure mode to avoid: the personal arc resolving before the civilizational arc peaks, or vice versa. If AJ and Twilight fully resolve their relationship anxiety and mutual trust while the war is still at its most dangerous, the personal track has flatlined while the civilizational track is still climbing — and the reader's investment drops on the personal level. The correct structure is that every major escalation on the civilizational track should /force/ a corresponding personal escalation. The fall of Stalliongrad (civilizational disaster) must also be a personal disaster for whoever is personally implicated in it. The Bluebell Spearhead decision (civilizational gamble) must also be a personal gamble for Applejack. The synchronized ratchet ensures the reader is invested at all levels simultaneously throughout. *The specific Minerva test:* At any point in the narrative, can a reader articulate both "what is the war situation right now, and is it worse or better than three chapters ago?" and "what is AJ's psychological state right now, and is it worse or better than three chapters ago?" If one of those is unclear, that track has stalled. Relentless Pursuit → Institutional Constraint as Obstacle, Not Excuse Minerva warns against passive characters who take breaks from their goal. The geopolitical scale introduces legitimate institutional constraints — alliances, politics, supply chains, the deliberate pace of a command economy — that can appear to excuse passivity. The professional solution is to design institutional constraints as /the obstacles to the goal/, not as reasons the character stops pursuing it. Applejack cannot charge forward on the Bluebell Spearhead because she lacks the tanks. This does not make her passive — it redirects her relentless pursuit into sourcing the tanks (Temberik crystals, Skyfall valves, the war bonds). Every scene where she is "waiting" for a resource must be a scene where she is also /actively working/ another angle of the same goal. The key extension is what could be called *parallel fronts of pursuit*: in a geopolitical epic, the protagonist cannot be relentlessly pursuing a single concrete goal at every moment because institutional reality prevents it. Instead, the reader should see her pursuing multiple fronts of the same overarching goal simultaneously — military, economic, political, diplomatic — so that the overall impression is relentlessness even when any single front is temporally blocked. Curiosity Hook → The Layered Revelation Architecture Minerva's curiosity hook operates on a single question. A geopolitical epic needs *simultaneous hooks at different scales*, each resolving at a different rate. The fabula currently has at least three active hook types: *Personal mystery hooks* (resolve fully within TLTT): What is the truth of Celestia's plan? Will AJ and Twilight survive their own psychological damage? These resolve through character arc. *Systemic mystery hooks* (resolve partially in TLTT, fully in prequels): How does the Changeling economy actually work? What was Chrysalis's original motivation? These resolve through piecemeal Codex revelation — the reader assembles the answer from fragments across scenes. *Structural mystery hooks* (structural irony — the reader assembles before the characters do): The reader gradually understands that the MEFO bill model is mathematically doomed /before/ Chrysalis acknowledges it. The reader understands Applejack's genuine leadership competence / before/ Applejack does. This is the most powerful hook type for third- person limited narration — dramatic irony where the reader's understanding outpaces the POV character's self-perception. The *management discipline* is: each hook type needs a /planted establishment/ (the moment the reader recognizes the question), a / maintenance strategy/ (how the question stays alive through scenes that don't answer it), and a /resolution calibration/ (how fully it resolves in TLTT vs. being left for prequels). The PlotPointCodexEntry system with its CodexUsageType enum is doing this for systemic hooks — Definition, Subversion, Failure are the stages of a systemic mystery's arc. The PlotPointCharacter system is doing this for personal hooks. The structural irony hooks are the least explicitly managed and most dependent on the author's craft. Core Wound → The Civilizational Wound as Macro-Analog Minerva's core wound: the character's deepest fear that the story forces them to confront. The extension for a geopolitical epic: *the civilization itself must have a structural wound that mirrors and amplifies the protagonist's personal wound*. Equestria's civilizational wound is the Stagnation of Harmony — a thousand years of suppressed ambition, false safety, and conditional love that has made the civilization simultaneously peaceful and defenseless. THE FIVE LAYERS: INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS AND WHY THEY FIT TLTT Where the Layers Actually Come From The five-layer framework was not invented for this project. It is a synthesis of distinct methodological traditions that converge on the same structural insight from different directions. Understanding the origins matters because it tells you why the framework has the shape it does, and when to override it with project-specific reasoning. *Layer 1 (World Truth / Ontology)* derives from the Secondary World tradition codified by Tolkien in "On Fairy-Stories." Tolkien's fundamental argument was that a fictional world must possess internal consistency independent of its narrative — the rules of Middle-earth are not invented to serve the plot; the plot is constrained by the rules. Brandon Sanderson extended this into explicit craft principles: the "Laws of Magic" framework and his cosmere building methodology, where world-systems are documented separately from any story that uses them. The Layer 1 claim-type is: /what is true, regardless of who knows it or when it is shown./ *Layer 2 (Objective Timeline)* is Russian Formalist fabula theory (Shklovsky, Tomashevsky, 1920s) applied as a documentation practice. The Formalists distinguished between fabula (the raw chronological sequence of events, as if narrated by an omniscient recorder with no aesthetic agenda) and syuzhet (the ordered, shaped presentation of those events to a reader). Tolkien's appendices are the most famous literary instantiation of Layer 2 as a document type — he wrote the history of Númenor as history, separate from any narrative that draws on it. George R.R. Martin's "The World of Ice and Fire" is another. The claim-type is: /what occurred, in what order, as observable fact./ *Layer 3 (Character Psychology)* derives from the character-first craft tradition, most articulately expressed by Robin Hobb and Robert McKee. Hobb famously refuses to plan plot — she argues that if characters are psychologically rigorous, they generate plot organically when placed in situations. McKee's "character revelation" framework (the distinction between what a character says they are and what they prove themselves to be under pressure) is the analytical lens for this layer. For ensemble works specifically, the theory of psychological interoperability — how different psychological architectures create different forms of friction and resonance when they interact — comes from showrunner writing-room practice, where character bibles are maintained separately from plot bibles. The claim-type is: /who this creature is, and why, independent of any event./ *Layer 4 (Syuzhet Architecture)* is the narrative delivery layer — the other half of the Russian Formalist pair. In practice, the discipline of managing what the reader knows and when comes most rigorously from screenwriting theory (Hitchcock's articulation of suspense vs. surprise, which depends entirely on the gap between reader knowledge and character knowledge) and from the tradition of the "unreliable narrator" in literary fiction. For third-person limited specifically, the key theorist is Henry James, whose prefaces to his novels constitute the first rigorous documentation of how limited perspective can be used to generate structural irony. The claim-type is: /how the story is experienced by the reader, scene by scene, from initiation to resolution./ *Layer 5 (Thematic Argument)* comes from the Aristotelian tradition: a story is an argument, and its ending is its conclusion. Ursula K. Le Guin's craft essays ("The Wave in the Mind," "Steering the Craft") and McKee's concept of the "controlling idea" are the most actionable modern formulations. The critical insight is that themes are not messages / about/ the world but /propositions/ the story tests against the world — the story dramatizes the conditions under which the proposition is true or false. The claim-type is: /what the story asserts about the nature of the world./ Why These Five and Not Some Other Number The layers are five because they correspond to five genuinely distinct / types of analytical question/ that require different epistemological modes: * Layer 1 requires systems thinking: how do mechanics interact? * Layer 2 requires historiographical thinking: what caused what, in what order? * Layer 3 requires psychological thinking: what does this creature fear, want, and misperceive? * Layer 4 requires rhetorical thinking: how is information structured to produce a specific effect in a reader? * Layer 5 requires philosophical thinking: what does this whole structure argue? No two of these questions can be answered by the same analytical mode. This is why mixing them in a single document produces friction — you are forced to context-switch between incompatible cognitive operations in rapid succession. Why They Are Specifically Correct for TLTT The five-layer framework is optimal for TLTT not just because it fits large-scale fiction generally but because it maps directly onto what Brian identified as his own intellectual method in the NotebookLM introspection: *historical materialism as narrative engine*. Historical materialism as a framework (in the broad, non-dogmatic sense that Brian is using it) already implies a specific causal chain: material conditions → historical events → cultural formations → individual psychology → individual action → narrative → meaning. This is a five-stage causal stack. The five layers of the story planner are its documentation equivalent: Materialist causal stage Corresponding layer Material conditions (what is physically/magically true about the world) Layer 1: World Truth Historical events (what has occurred as a result of those conditions) Layer 2: Objective Timeline Cultural formations → individual psychology (what those conditions and events made creatures into) Layer 3: Character Psychology Individual action → narrative (how those creatures act and how that action is presented) Layer 4: Syuzhet Architecture Meaning (what the whole structure argues about the nature of the world) Layer 5: Thematic Argument The framework was not arbitrary — it was the natural formalization of a methodology that already existed implicitly in the project's intellectual foundations. WHAT MAKES TLTT AN EPIC The Literary Definition, Applied "Epic" is one of the most overused terms in popular discourse and one of the most specific in literary tradition. Clarifying what it actually means — and demonstrating that TLTT meets the bar — provides the framework for understanding which external advice is applicable and which is not. The classical epic (Homer, Virgil) has formal properties that are largely irrelevant to prose fiction: invocation of the Muse, in medias res opening, epic similes, divine machinery, the hero's journey through the underworld. What persists into the modern epic as structural requirements is more fundamental: *1. Civilizational Scale*: The epic's central conflict operates at the level of civilization — a whole people's way of life is at stake, not just an individual's fate. The Iliad is not about Achilles's honor; it is about what kind of civilization survives the Trojan War. War and Peace is not about Pierre's spiritual development; it is about whether a certain Russian civilization survives Napoleon. TLTT is explicitly about whether the Stagnation of Harmony can evolve into the Equestrian Republic — the fate of a civilization's entire political and cultural form. *2. Historical Embeddedness*: Epic characters are not free agents; they are creatures of historical forces larger than themselves. Achilles cannot simply walk away from the war. Andromache cannot simply leave Troy. In TLTT, neither AJ nor Twilight can simply go home — they are embedded in historical forces (the Changeling economy's mathematical necessity for expansion, the century of Stagnation that left Equestria unprepared) that constrain their choices even as they make them. *3. The Founding Myth Structure*: Epics are typically origin stories — they explain how a civilization became what it is. The Aeneid explains Rome. The Mahabharata explains dharma. TLTT is explicitly about the founding of the Equestrian Republic — the event that ends the Stagnation of Harmony and creates the post-war world. The prequels then function as the historical archaeology of that founding: how the preconditions came to exist. *4. Multiple Simultaneous Scales of Conflict*: Epics do not operate at a single scale. They are simultaneously personal (AJ's imposter syndrome), political (the Republic vs. the Monarchy), military (the war), civilizational (Harmonic Capitalism vs. the Predator's Dilemma), and philosophical (what does friendship actually mean in a world where predators exist). The narrative must be coherent at all these scales simultaneously — events must be legible as both personal and civilizational. *5. Thematic Ambition Beyond Entertainment*: Epics are not merely stories that happen to be long. They are interventions in cultural discourse. The Iliad was asking serious questions about heroism and its costs that the Greek cultural consensus had not fully confronted. Les Misérables was asking questions about justice, poverty, and the law. TLTT is asking: can the values of friendship and harmony survive contact with the materialist reality of the world, and if so, on what terms? TLTT meets all five criteria with unusual rigor for a fanfiction project. It is a genuine epic. How TLTT Differs from Other Genres and Works in Its Space *vs. Standard Fantasy Epic (Tolkien, Sanderson, Jordan):* The standard fantasy epic is typically structured around a chosen one or prophesied event that drives the narrative toward cosmic resolution. Magic is predominantly a force of good vs. evil rather than a technological- economic system. Its core claims are: 1. The /base/ — the material conditions of economic production (who owns the means of production, how labor is organized, what technologies are available) — is the primary determinant of human social organization. 2. History is driven by class struggle: conflicts between classes defined by their relationship to the means of production. 4. This process is directional and teleological: history moves through specific stages (primitive communism → slavery → feudalism → capitalism → socialism → communism) driven by contradictions within each mode of production. The reader's act of assembly creates ownership. They didn't receive the conclusion; they produced it. It feels true in a way that received conclusions never do. The failure mode at every scale is the same: the author doesn't trust the reader to assemble the evidence correctly, so they add the explicit statement alongside the evidence. The explicit statement then does two things: it renders the evidence redundant (why did we need all those scenes if you were just going to tell us?), and it signals that the author is managing the reader's conclusions, which makes the reader defensive rather than engaged. ------------------------------------------------------------------------ The Scale Hierarchy The principle operates at five distinct scales, and discipline at each scale is what separates competent from excellent craft. *Scale 1 — Thematic (the proposition level).* The story tests a claim about the world rather than delivering it. The reader watches the test run and arrives at the conclusion through witnessing. As established: "industry is neither good nor evil, it is an accelerant" is a message. The full structural proof — Chrysalis deploys it one way, the Republic deploys it another, the charitostatic effect is the physical mechanism that produces different outcomes — is the proposition being tested. The reader arrives at the conclusion through the logic of the demonstration. *Scale 2 — Character arc.* A character's psychological transformation is shown through the accumulation of decisions under pressure, not stated through introspective summary. The failure mode is "she realized she had been wrong all along" — the author naming the arc instead of dramatizing it. Applejack accepting the general's star is the proof of her arc's completion. The narration does not need to say she overcame her imposter syndrome. The action proves it. *Scale 3 — Scene.* The scene accomplishes its structural work (advances the thread, demonstrates the theme, develops the character) through what happens, not through what characters or narration state about what is happening. The trust argument is the subtext. The failure mode is a character breaking the surface to say what the argument is actually about. The correct form is that the logistics argument reaches a resolution that is emotionally legible as a trust resolution without either character naming the emotional register. *Scale 5 — Sentence and word.* This is where "show, don't tell" is most commonly taught and most narrowly applied, and it is the least important of the five scales. But this scale is also the most frequently over-corrected. The first is the mechanical substitution of sensory description for abstract statement, treating the advice as a find-and-replace operation: remove all instances of "she felt afraid" and replace with physical symptoms of fear. This produces flat, clinical prose where the reader is given data points about physical states and must infer the emotional content — which is the reverse of the problem it was supposed to solve. The reader is now doing assembly work on sensory data when they should be experiencing an emotion. The second misapplication is treating Scale 5 as the primary level of the principle, which means writers spend enormous energy on sentence- level craft while leaving Scales 1 through 4 untended. A story with beautifully rendered sensory prose and a theme that is a message, characters whose arcs are named rather than demonstrated, and scenes that explain themselves is a story that tells rather than shows at every level that actually matters, regardless of how good the sentences are. The correct hierarchy is almost the inverse of how the advice is usually taught. Scale 1 (proposition) is the foundation. If the thematic architecture is a test rather than a message, everything below it has the structural permission to do the same. If the thematic architecture is a message, no amount of sensory precision at Scale 5 will compensate — the reader will feel managed regardless of how beautiful the sentences are. ------------------------------------------------------------------------ Word Choice Specifically Word choice is not merely sensory precision. It operates on three registers simultaneously: meaning (denotation), connotation (the emotional and cultural associations the word carries), and rhythm (the phonetic and stress pattern of the word within its sentence). All three registers communicate simultaneously with the reader, and dissonance between them is immediately felt as wrongness even when the reader cannot name it. The practical implication: the right word for a moment is the word whose denotation, connotation, and rhythm all agree with what the moment is doing. "She was tired" and "she was exhausted" and "she was depleted" and "she was spent" all denote the same physical state. Their connotations differ: exhausted has an honorable quality (effort was made), depleted has a resource-scarcity quality (something was consumed), spent has a financial quality (something was exchanged for something else), tired is neutral. Their rhythms differ: spent is a single hard beat, depleted spreads the weight over three syllables, exhausted falls with emphasis on the middle. For a scene where Applejack has given everything she has and the cost was worth it, "spent" is precisely right in a way that "tired" is not, and the rightness is in the connotation and rhythm as much as the denotation. The micro level matters, but it is downstream of everything else. A sentence with perfect word choice embedded in a scene that tells rather than shows is still a scene that tells. THE ORTHOGONAL ARCS QUESTION A character's arcs are not orthogonal. They are the same arc expressed through different domains simultaneously. This is the correct intuition embedded in your observation that "it's all coherent" — and it is worth being precise about why. A character has one psychological architecture. That architecture expresses itself under pressure in every domain the story puts them into: professional decisions, relationships, ideology, physical action. What looks like separate arcs — Applejack's imposter syndrome arc, her ideological arc about industry, her romantic arc with Twilight, her political arc about the Republic — are all the same underlying wound (suppressed legitimate ambition) being exposed and tested by different situations. The romantic arc and the political arc are not orthogonal; they are the same arc in different keys. Twilight accepting her own power is both the condition for the Republic existing and the condition for the TwiJack relationship reaching its full depth. You cannot resolve one without the other because they are the same psychological event. The practical consequence: a character should have one Character Track A entry that describes the unified psychological architecture, and the multiple apparent "arcs" are tracked through Story Threads — each thread is one domain of expression for that underlying structure. AJ's imposter syndrome manifests in the "Establishing the Equestrian Republic" thread, the "TwiJack" thread, and the "Honesty vs Poseurs" thematic argument simultaneously. WHAT MAKES A CHAPTER A CHAPTER The Formal Answer A chapter is the unit of *reader experience between commitment points*. It is the amount of story a reader consumes in one sitting before they decide whether to continue. This means a chapter has two distinct structural obligations: it must be internally coherent as an experience, and it must end in a state that makes continuing feel necessary. The internal coherence requirement means a chapter needs a *local question* — a specific narrative question that the chapter opens, develops, and either resolves or decisively transforms before it ends. This is distinct from the overarching questions of the story threads. The chapter's local question is the unit of attention the reader commits to when they begin reading. It may be as concrete as "will the Temberik agree to the mine?" or as atmospheric as "what does Applejack's first day back feel like after months apart?" The chapter's scenes are the stages of that question's development. The commitment point requirement means a chapter must end in a state that is *narratively unstable in a forward direction* — the local question was answered (or transformed) in a way that makes a new question urgent. This is not the same as a cliffhanger, which is a cheap mechanical device. It is the natural forward pressure of story logic: the answer to this chapter's question immediately opens the next chapter's question, and the reader perceives that opening as irresistible. Grounded in the Story Planner's Language In the planner's terms, a chapter is a *container for a local TensionPhase arc that is complete within the chapter's bounds*. The arc must move from some form of Situation or Spark through Escalation and LocalClimax to at minimum an Impact — ideally reaching Resolution of the local question while generating the Spark for the next chapter's question. The chapter's plot points, read in TensionPhase order, should describe a coherent tension curve. If you read just the TensionPhase values of a chapter's plot points in sequence and they do not form a recognizable arc shape — rising through Escalation to a LocalClimax and then falling through Impact and Resolution — the chapter is either incomplete, overstuffed, or has been assembled from scenes that belong to different local questions. The chapter's *local question* maps most precisely onto the chapter- level Stakes field. This is where the chapter's reason for existing as a unit is stated. It answers: "What question is the reader living inside for the duration of this chapter?" Not what happens — that is the Summary field — but what the reader needs to find out.
