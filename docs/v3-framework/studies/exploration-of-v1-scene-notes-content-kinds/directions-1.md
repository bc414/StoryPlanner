## What you are given

One plot point from the archived planning database of a long work of fiction: its title, its
chapter and position, its synopsis, and its outcome and stakes where they hold text; then
every link it has, to a character, a theme, a story thread or a codex entry of world lore,
each under its kind and name, with the settings the author chose on it in brackets and the
link's own text, "(no text)" where it has none. The settings are labels picked from fixed
lists; they say what the author selected, not what the scene does. The synopsis and each
link's text are free-form boxes the author wrote into over time: they run to several
paragraphs and one box may hold several separate things. You have nothing else of the plan:
not the other plot points, not the linked entities' own notes, not the story's text.

## How to read

Read the whole item, the links' text as well as the synopsis. Go through it passage by
passage, a passage being a stretch that does one thing; a paragraph may hold more than one,
and one passage may run across paragraphs.

Ask of each passage what kind of content it is. Six kinds are asked for by name. A fact of
the story's world: something that is so, whether or not this scene shows it — an event, a
date, a piece of history, an institution, a law of the world, a character's psychology, a
relationship. A character's belief or perception: what someone in the story thinks, feels,
assumes or takes to be true, whether or not it is. Scene content seen from someone's side:
what happens here as it is experienced by a particular character — what they notice, how
things land on them, what they do about it. An authorial reading: what some fact or event
means, what it shows, what it says about the world or the story, written from outside the
story. A fragment of prose: words drafted as they would appear on the page, a line of
dialogue or a sentence of narration. A note to self: a task, a doubt, a question left open,
a reminder, a decision still to be made. Report every other kind you find as well, named in
your own terms.

Two further things about a passage. First, how it is carried: a fact of the story's world
may be stated plainly as true, or it may be put inside quoted speech, inside a letter, a
document, a broadcast or a report, or inside a character's thought. Second, where the
passage gives a character's belief: whether the truth that belief is wrong about is in this
same item, and whether the item names a scene, a moment or a date at which the character is
corrected. Record both only from what the words show.

## What to produce

- passages: list of line, one per passage, six parts separated by a bar: where in the item, the section or the link by its kind and name | kind, one of fact of the world, belief or perception, scene content from a side, authorial reading, prose fragment, note to self, or your own name for any other | what it holds, in your words | what marks it, a few words of the item allowed | carried, one of plainly, inside speech, inside a letter or document, inside a thought, or none where the passage is not a fact of the story's world | paired, for a passage giving a character's belief: truth present or truth absent, then a semicolon and the correction the item names, or none named; none where the passage gives no belief
- mixed: line, whether one box of this item — the synopsis, the outcome, the stakes, or one link's text — holds more than one kind, naming which boxes do

## Never

Never name a position inside a box: no line or paragraph numbers, and no quotation used only
to say where. Never supply a fact of the story's world that the item does not hold, and
never judge whose words a passage is in. Never say what the item should have held.
